into solutions, we need to understand why a problem exists in the first place. Not just at the
surface, but at the root. A simple but powerful tool for this is the five whys exercise. The premise
is straightforward. Start with a problem you can observe, then ask why five times. Each answer peels
back a layer, moving you from symptom to cause. Many government digital products start in the wrong
place. They begin with a solution in mind. We've heard requests like these. We are not using enough
AI. AI is often a means to an end, not an end in itself. The real issue might be that officers
struggle to make decisions from large unruly data sets. We might hear that we don't have a CRM
platform. But why do we need one? The real issue might be cumbersome processes causing long wait
times for citizens. Our product is reaching end of life. But what's really at stake is that some
citizens and officers will lose access to some critical service. When we skip proper problem
definition, we risk building the wrong thing entirely. On time, on budget, but completely unhelpful.
Here's a simple example to illustrate the five whys in action. Suppose you were late to work late
this morning. Why? You were late for work. Why were you late? You woke up late. Because your alarm
didn't go off because the battery was flat because you forgot to replace it. The fix isn't a new
alarm clock. It's replacing the battery. A small intervention solves the entire problem. Now let's
apply this to a government context. Applicants can't access grants. Why? Because applications are
taking too long to process. Officers are spending too much time going back and forth with applicant
applicants. Applications are com- coming in incomplete because the form is full of internal jargon
because it wasn't designed around how the agency works, but how applicants think. The last answer
here is the real problem to solve. Not processing time, not back and forth, the simple form itself.
In practice, problems rarely have a single root cause. There are usually several contributing
factors, and that's okay. Strike a balance between a meaningful and achievable problem, closer to
the root cause, and within time and resource constraints. The goal isn't to find a perfect answer.
It's to identify the causes that are within your control and strongly connected to the outcomes you
want to achieve. Before any initiative, start with the why. The five whys exercise gets you past the
surface-level problems and closer to the real one. You don't always have to fix the last why in the
chain. Focus on the ones within your control. Here's the first exercise. Think of a problem that
your division is currently facing. Ask why five times, and figure out which reasons sit within your
sphere of influence. In the next section, we'll take what we've uncovered and turn it into a clear
well-crafted problem statement.
