problem statement, useful metrics, and a handle on our risks. Now for another key aspect to think
of. What does it actually feel like to use what we build? Good customer experience isn't a nice
have. It's especially important when our services are compulsory because citizens don't have options
to go elsewhere. The best digital experiences share one quality. The product does the thinking so
the user doesn't have to. If your service requires a lengthy tutorial to navigate, it's a signal.
It's not about your users, it's about your design. Two government products illustrate this well.
Parking SG calculates parking fees automatically so I don't have to guess how long I'll take. It
lets me extend my sessions remotely so I don't have to rush back to my car. It refunds partial
sessions so I never get penalized for leaving early. Each of these features solves a specific
problem and real frustration. The result is an experience that's 10 times better than what came
before. Redeem SG serves three different users at once. Campaign organizers, consumers, and
merchants, each with different needs and different definitions of a good experience. The team
couldn't make everyone equally happy, but it's about understanding the core problem to solve for
each one to make smart trade-offs. Get the essentials right first and build a road map for
everything else. That clarity is what enabled them to deliver value quickly. A useful tool for
thinking about this is Airbnb's 11-star framework. An exercise that helps us calibrate what good
might look like and feel like by pushing beyond what we normally consider acceptable. Here is how it
works. Let's start at one star. You knock at the door, the one answers. Two stars is when you wait
20 minutes for the host to show up. Often, when users give five stars, all they mean is I knocked,
someone opened the door, and they let me in. That's the baseline. Let's keep going. If a user gives
six out of five stars, perhaps it's because the host welcomed you warmly, showed you around, the
place is clean and well stocked. Seven stars, the host learns that you like surfing, leaves a
surfboard and a car, and recommends you the best local restaurants. Let's push further. Eight stars,
a private chef. Nine stars, a personalized itinerary waiting on the table. 10 stars, a limousine
from the airport, a press conference at the door. 11 stars, Elon Musk shows up at the game and tells
you you're going to space. Now, the 10 and 11 star experiences aren't the goal. They're a prompt.
Somewhere between five and 11 stars lies an experience that's genuinely worth building and feasible.
The exercise forces us to think beyond good enough and to find out what's both ambitious and
achievable. Airbnb apply this thinking directly into their product. They rate hosts on friendliness,
accuracy, check-in, communications, and location. And they surface super hosts, so that guests can
find great experience without having to search too hard. The product does the work. Good design
means that the product thinks so the user doesn't have to. This standard applies whether your
service is optional or mandatory. Perhaps especially so when it's mandatory. Here's your exercise.
For the problem statements, what does an 11-star experience look like? Walk through the framework,
then ask yourself what you can realistically deliver and what would get you closest to it. In the
