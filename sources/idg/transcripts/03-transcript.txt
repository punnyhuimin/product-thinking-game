the root cause. Now, we need to articulate it clearly in a way that aligns your team, grounds the

A useful way to write one is the four C's framework: clarity, consequence, cause, and confirmation.
Clarity is where you define the problem precisely. Who are the people affected? What are they trying
to do? What's broken about the current experience? How severe is the problem? How many people and
how often? Consequence addresses what happens if you don't solve this. Every problem statement
should carry a sense of stakes. If the answer is not much, it may not be worth solving. Cause can
expect the five whys work. Why does this problem exist? A good problem statement doesn't just
describe what's wrong. It points to what's right. Confirmation grounds everything in data. What
evidence do we have that this problem is real, significant, and worth prioritizing? Together, the
framework gives you a clear, defensible problem statement. Not a wish list, not a solution in
disguise. Here's how it might look like when you map it against the four C's. Clarity. Who is
affected? What are they trying to do? And what's broken? Citizens need to access multiple government
services each year. To do so, they're required to fill in the same personal information more than 15
times across different platforms. Each form takes 20 to 30 minutes to complete, and many give up
before finishing, missing out on benefits they're entitled to. Consequence. What happens if you
don't fix this? Citizens are losing access to support they qualify for. Trust in government services
erodes, and the burden falls heaviest on those least equipped to navigate complex processes. Cause.
Why does this happen? Government services are built independently by different agencies at different
times. There is no shared data layer, so every service starts from scratch, asking the same
questions again. Confirmation. Does the data support this? 68% of transactions are abandoned due to
repeat repeated form-filling frustration. That's approximately 204,000 incomplete transactions out
of 300,000 annually. Now, there is always one main problem statement covering all four dimensions.
If there are many, perhaps they should be addressed separately. Notice what changed. The focus
shifted from the tool to the people, from the technology to the impact. That's the difference a
proper problem statement makes. The best solutions are built on the clearest problems, not on the
technology available or the features someone requested, but on the precise, evidence-backed
understanding of what's actually wrong and who it affects. Before your next project kickoff, try
writing a problem statement using the 4 C's framework. If you can't complete all four themes, you're
probably not ready to build yet. Here's the second exercise. For the problem space you've identified
