problem statement and metrics to track, but before we build anything, we need to ask, what could
possibly go wrong? And what's the cheapest way to find out? This section is about dreaming big and
starting small. Every product faces three types of risks. There is market risk. Will anyone actually
use this? And even if they do, will it solve the problem? There are technical risks. Is this too
complex or too costly to build relative to the business impact it delivers? There are team risks. Do
we have the right capabilities in the team to pull this off? There are always risks. If a project
seems entirely risk-free, it's a sign that someone isn't looking hard enough. Most digital products
go through several phases to de-risk. A proof of concept test whether the solution works. It's
usually scoped to a single user segment with a single use case. The proof of value phase test
whether something can scale. We now scale to more user segments to solve more problems. The scale
phase is about reaching as many users as possible, and maturity is where we continue improving the
product over time. Building a product in stages, scaling isn't about building all the parts before
anything works. When we want to move people from point A to point B, we don't just build the wheels,
the chassis, and then the body, and finally a car. We start with a skateboard, then a scooter, a
bicycle, a motorcycle, and finally a car. At every stage, users have something functional. At every
stage, we are learning something. Amazon started with books. Facebook launched at a single
university. Airbnb started with the founders own spare room. Netflix started with DVD rentals. Grab
started with taxis. None of them started with the full product we see today. They started with one
problem solved well. A good example from the government is the health appointment booking system.
The problem, pneumococcal was one of the leading causes of death in Singapore. It is preventable by
a vaccine, but the government rates in high-risk groups were low. The hypothesis, by making it
easier to book, more people will get vaccinated. The main risk was a market risk. Would people
actually book online? To test it, they did the simplest possible version. A form SG form feeding
into an Excel sheet with a product manager manually calling clinics and sending confirmation via
email. Not scalable, but that wasn't the point. It was the lowest way to validate the approach. They
also ran an AB test. The control group received a standard letter having to find the clinics
themselves. The treatment group received a letter with a QR code linking to the booking flow. At the
end of 2 weeks, there were zero appointments from the control group and 24 from the treatment group.
With more completions than the original test subjects. One variable with clear results. To address
market risk, get the product in front of your real users as early as possible. You want to be
roughly correct early, not precisely wrong later. When we observe users, watch what they do, not
just what they say. The behavior often reveals more than feedback forms. To address team risk,
structure your team for speed, focus, and flexibility. Speed, so that your team can be learning from
decisions quickly without getting stuck in processes. Focus, so that the team can stay locked in to
the underlying problem, not just the features. Flexibility, so that you can iterate when the
evidence tells you to. Two principles underpin this. First, bring together policy what's and tech
expertise in one team with actual levers to act. Second, establish clear accountabilities for each
role. Ambiguity about who owns what is where good projects start to stall. Each initiative carries
risks. Usually some combination of market, technical, and or team risk. The goal isn't to eliminate
risk entirely, but to identify the biggest risk early and to find the lowest cost way to test our
assumptions before we over invest. Start with a proof of concept, learn from it, then scale what
works. Here's the question to reflect on. What is the key risk that could prevent your initiative
from succeeding, and what's the simplest experiment you could run to find out?
