clear problem statement. Now, we need to measure progress. Without the right metrics, we can't tell
whether we're actually solving the problem or just being busy. In this section, we cover three tools
for defining better metrics. First, SMART, a framework for defining what good metrics looks like.
Second, leading and lagging indicators to help us find the most appropriate metric to track. And
third, value for class ratio, the whole of government metric that helps us assess returns on
investment. Metrics matter because they keep us honest. They tell us how well we're solving the
problem and to what extent. Without them, we're building on instinct, and instincts are known hardly
hold up in a funding review. SMART metrics. Good metrics share five properties. They are specific,
measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. Specific. Making the website better is not very
specific. Improving the click-through rates from 20% to 25% is. Measurable. Improving citizen
happiness is not measurable. Net promoter score is measurable. Achievable. What one engineer can
deliver in the sprint is very different from what 10 engineers can deliver in a year. Set targets
that that are ambitious yet grounded. Relevant. If NPS increases, users are likely happier. If page
load time drops, the experience is likely better. The metric should move when the problem is being
solved. Time-bound. What's achievable in 3 months looks very different from what's achievable in 3
years. Anchor your targets to a timeline. One useful signal. If you're struggling to identify a
single right metric, the problem statement is probably too broad. Narrow down the problem statement.
Leading and lagging indicators. Not all metrics are equally easy to measure. That's where leading
and lagging indicators come in. Leading indicators are early signals. Measurable frequently and
precisely, but perhaps further from the actual outcome. Lagging indicators sit closer to the real
problem, but tend to take longer to surface and may be harder to pin down. Your key metric usually
lies somewhere in between. Here's an example using the teachers portal. On one hand, we can easily
track the number of teachers on board daily. It's easy to measure, but perhaps not very telling.
Moving further on, you know how many teachers used a specific feature, how many crafted a message
within a specific time limit, how many campaigns were approved quickly, and finally, how much time
did the teachers actually save? The last indicator is probably closest to the real problem, but it
might only be measurable quarterly. The value of tracking the earlier indicators is that it gives
you faster feedback. If a new feature we released shows low adoption rate in week two, you don't
have to wait till the end of the quarter to conclude that it probably isn't saving anyone's time.
Value cost ratio. The value cost ratio or VCR is a metric adopted across the whole of government. It
measures the units of value generated per dollar spent. VCR isn't a verdict. It doesn't tell you
whether a number is good or bad in isolation, but it does ask the right and enable the right
questions. How much value do we expect from an extra dollar of investment and what needs to change
for the ratio to improve? Good metrics are smart. Leading and lagging indicators help us find the
right ones to track. Value cost ratio helps us answer the right questions. They matter in every
conversation. Here's a third exercise. For your problem statement, identify your most appropriate
metric. Map out the leading and lagging indicators to find it, then derive your current value cost
ratio. In the next section, we look at assumptions and risks.
