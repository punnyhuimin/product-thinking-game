Problems before solutions

How to tell when someone is pitching a solution instead of describing a problem, and what to ask instead.

By the end you'll be able to look at a project idea and say whether it starts from a problem or a solution. You'll also be able to tell whether its success measure is an output or an outcome.

Where do you start?

IDG opens the pathway with this scenario: "You have a budget, a team, and a deadline. Your director says, 'We need AI.' Your team says, 'We need a super app.' Where do you start?"

IDG's answer: "neither."All quotes in this lesson come from IDG's Guide 1 video unless marked otherwise. A transcript is saved at sources/idg/transcripts/01-transcript.txt.

"Great digital products don't start with solutions, they start with problems." IDG, Guide 1: Understanding the Problem

"We need AI" and "we need a super app" are what IDG calls "solutions dressed up as problems." They name a technology before anyone has said what's going wrong, or for whom. IDG suggests asking three questions instead:

  1. Who are our users?
  2. What are they trying to do?
  3. What is getting in their way?

If a statement answers those three questions, it describes a problem. If it skips straight to a tool, it's a solution in disguise.

Why this matters: a team that skips problem definition risks building something that IDG describes as "on time, on budget, but completely unhelpful."From the Guide 2 video.

IDG's three principles

IDG says "three things help us" create the conditions for teams to succeed:

  1. Focus on the problem, not the solution. The idea above.
  2. Chase outcomes, not outputs. More on this below.
  3. Integrate policy, ops, and tech. "No initiative succeeds when these three work in silos." Policy sets the rules, operations runs the service, and tech builds the systems. They have to work as one team.In Guide 5, IDG adds that this team needs "actual levers to act" and "clear accountabilities for each role".

Output or outcome?

"A website with five buttons is an output. More citizens completing transactions is an outcome."

An output is something your team produces: an app, a feature, a training session. An outcome is the change that results for people. Outputs are easy to count, but outcomes are what IDG asks you to chase. Success is "the change achieved, not the features delivered."IDG learning objective 2, on the overview page.

Start narrow

IDG's examples: Grab started with one goal, making taxis "safer, more reliable, and easier to book". Amazon started with books. Netflix started by making DVD rentals cheaper and more reliable. Each is huge today, but IDG's point is: "Solve one problem first, then scale."
